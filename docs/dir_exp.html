Mixed Dirichlet–Kirchhoff diffusion: derivation of the matrix solvers

Experiments · BRASIL theory and matrix-solver functions

This document derives the matrix schemes used in the star-graph experiments with prescribed Dirichlet values at selected leaves. It follows the notation and the step-by-step derivation in brasilfunc.Rmd. We first derive the ordinary diffusion scheme, corresponding to \(\alpha=2\), and then derive the spectral fractional scheme and its rational matrix solver. The latter uses a harmonic lifting to specify the meaning of nonhomogeneous Dirichlet data. Knitting this document explains the schemes; it does not run the experiments or modify their scripts. The companion file dir_exp.tex is retained.

Diffusion equations with mixed vertex conditions

Let \(\Gamma=(\mathcal V,\mathcal E)\) be a compact connected metric graph. Partition its vertices into a nonempty set \(\mathcal V_D\) of prescribed Dirichlet vertices and the remaining Kirchhoff vertices \(\mathcal V_K=\mathcal V\setminus\mathcal V_D\). We write \[ L_D=\kappa^2-\Delta_{\Gamma,D},\qquad \beta=\alpha/2,\qquad \nu>0,\qquad \kappa\ge0. \] Here \(\Delta_{\Gamma,D}\) is the Laplacian with homogeneous Dirichlet conditions on \(\mathcal V_D\) and Kirchhoff conditions on \(\mathcal V_K\). Its domain is \[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal D(\Delta_{\Gamma,D}) =\bigl\{\phi\in C(\Gamma):\;&\phi|_e\in H^2(e) \text{ for every }e\in\mathcal E,\quad \phi(v)=0\text{ for }v\in\mathcal V_D,\\ &\sum_{e\in\mathcal E_v}\partial_e\phi(v)=0 \text{ for }v\in\mathcal V_K\bigr\}. \end{aligned} \] The derivatives are directed from each vertex into its incident edges. Every endpoint incidence is counted. At an unprescribed leaf, the Kirchhoff condition reduces to a homogeneous Neumann condition.

For \(\alpha=2\), the state equation is \[\begin{equation} \label{dir:eq:ordinary} \tag{1} \left\{ \begin{aligned} \partial_tu+\nu(\kappa^2u-u'')&=f, &&\text{on each edge, }0<t<T,\\ u(v,t)&=g_v(t),&&v\in\mathcal V_D,\\ \sum_{e\in\mathcal E_v}\partial_eu(v,t)&=0, &&v\in\mathcal V_K,\\ u(s,0)&=u_0(s),&&s\in\Gamma. \end{aligned} \right. \end{equation}\] The state is continuous at graph vertices. With \(\nu=1\) and \(\kappa^2=c_0\), this is the state equation (5.2) in Stoll and Winkler (2021). Only the state equation is used here; the values \(g_v\) are given functions.

Unlike the all-Kirchhoff setting in brasilfunc.Rmd, we may use \(\kappa=0\): the nonempty Dirichlet set removes the constant nullspace. The scripts use \(c_0=0\), \(\nu=1\), and the matrix named K in the scripts is the matrix denoted by \(\mathbf L\) below.

Function spaces and weak formulation

Define \[ \begin{aligned} V&=\{v\in C(\Gamma):v|_e\in H^1(e) \text{ for every }e\in\mathcal E\},\\ V_D&=\{v\in V:v(v_D)=0\text{ for every }v_D\in\mathcal V_D\}, \end{aligned} \] and introduce the unscaled bilinear form \[ \mathfrak a(u,v)=\kappa^2(u,v)_{L_2(\Gamma)} +(u',v')_{L_2(\Gamma)}. \] All integrals are sums of arclength integrals over the edges. Multiplication of the edge equation by \(\phi\in V_D\) and integration by parts gives \[\begin{equation} \label{dir:eq:weak} \tag{2} (\partial_tu,\phi)_{L_2(\Gamma)} +\nu\mathfrak a(u,\phi)=(f,\phi)_{L_2(\Gamma)}, \qquad \phi\in V_D, \qquad u|_{\mathcal V_D}=g. \end{equation}\] Indeed, the endpoint terms at a Kirchhoff vertex combine into its flux sum and vanish. The endpoint terms at a Dirichlet vertex vanish because the test function is zero there. The Kirchhoff condition is therefore incorporated naturally through the weak formulation.

We assume the smooth boundary functions used by the experiments. In particular, \(\dot g\) is meaningful in the lifting formulas below. The arbitrary \(L_2\) boundary data considered in the control paper require a very weak formulation and additional analysis.

Numerical scheme for ordinary diffusion: \(\alpha=2\)

Step 1: Introduce the finite element basis and matrices

Let \(V_h\subset V\) be the continuous piecewise linear finite element space on a graph mesh indexed by \(h\), and set \(V_{h,D}=V_h\cap V_D\). Using the same basis notation as in brasilfunc.Rmd, write \[ U_h(s,t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_h}u_i(t)\psi_h^i(s). \] The mass and stiffness matrices are \[\begin{equation} \label{dir:eq:matrices} \tag{3} \mathbf C_{i,j}=(\psi_h^j,\psi_h^i)_{L_2(\Gamma)},\qquad \mathbf G_{i,j}=({\psi_h^j}',{\psi_h^i}')_{L_2(\Gamma)},\qquad \mathbf L=\kappa^2\mathbf C+\mathbf G. \end{equation}\] The coefficient \(\nu\) remains outside \(\mathbf L\), as it does in brasilfunc.Rmd. On a mesh interval of length \(h_e\), the local matrices are \[ \mathbf C_e=\frac{h_e}{6} \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad \mathbf G_e=\frac1{h_e} \begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}. \] Their assembled matrices share one degree of freedom at each common vertex. Consequently the finite element state is continuous there. We use the consistent mass matrix, without mass lumping.

Step 2: Separate free coefficients from prescribed coefficients

Let \(D\) be the set of mesh indices corresponding to \(\mathcal V_D\), and let \(F\) contain all other mesh indices. In particular, \(F\) contains the edge-interior nodes, the center of the star, and the unprescribed leaves. Conceptually reorder the coefficient vector and matrices as \[ \mathbf U=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf U_F\\\mathbf U_D\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf C=\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf C_{FF}&\mathbf C_{FD}\\ \mathbf C_{DF}&\mathbf C_{DD} \end{pmatrix},\qquad \mathbf L=\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf L_{FF}&\mathbf L_{FD}\\ \mathbf L_{DF}&\mathbf L_{DD} \end{pmatrix}. \] Because the basis is nodal, imposing the boundary condition means \[ \mathbf U_D(t)=\mathbf g(t), \qquad \mathbf g(t)=(g_v(t))_{v\in\mathcal V_D}. \] Thus the solution is sought in an affine finite element space, while the test functions belong to the homogeneous space \(V_{h,D}\). There are equations only for the free indices.

Define the forcing load by \[ \mathbf F_i(t)=(f(t),\psi_h^i)_{L_2(\Gamma)}. \] Choosing \(\phi=\psi_h^i\), \(i\in F\), in (2) gives \[ \sum_{j\in F}C_{ij}\dot u_j +\sum_{j\in D}C_{ij}\dot g_j +\nu\sum_{j\in F}L_{ij}u_j +\nu\sum_{j\in D}L_{ij}g_j=F_i. \] Collecting the free coefficients on the left yields \[\begin{equation} \label{dir:eq:freeode} \tag{4} \mathbf C_{FF}\dot{\mathbf U}_F +\nu\mathbf L_{FF}\mathbf U_F =\mathbf F_F-\mathbf C_{FD}\dot{\mathbf g} -\nu\mathbf L_{FD}\mathbf g. \end{equation}\] This is the consequence of substituting the prescribed coefficients. It explains why overwriting boundary entries after an unconstrained solve is insufficient: their contributions must also enter the free node equations.

Step 3: Discretize time and derive the boundary corrections

Let \(t_k=k\tau\), \(k=0,\ldots,N\), with \(\tau=T/N\), and write \[ \delta\mathbf U_F^{k+1} =\frac{\mathbf U_F^{k+1}-\mathbf U_F^k}{\tau},\qquad \delta\mathbf g^{k+1} =\frac{\mathbf g^{k+1}-\mathbf g^k}{\tau}. \] As in brasilfunc.Rmd, use the time-averaged forcing \[ f^{k+1}=\frac1\tau\int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}}f(t)\,dt,\qquad F_i^{k+1}=(f^{k+1},\psi_h^i)_{L_2(\Gamma)}. \] Backward Euler applied to (4) gives \[ \mathbf C_{FF}\delta\mathbf U_F^{k+1} +\nu\mathbf L_{FF}\mathbf U_F^{k+1} =\mathbf F_F^{k+1} -\mathbf C_{FD}\delta\mathbf g^{k+1} -\nu\mathbf L_{FD}\mathbf g^{k+1}. \] Multiply by \(\tau\) and move the old free state to the right: \[\begin{equation} \label{dir:eq:ordinarybe} \tag{5} \boxed{\begin{aligned} (\mathbf C_{FF}+\tau\nu\mathbf L_{FF})\mathbf U_F^{k+1} ={}&\mathbf C_{FF}\mathbf U_F^k+\tau\mathbf F_F^{k+1}\\ &-\mathbf C_{FD}(\mathbf g^{k+1}-\mathbf g^k) -\tau\nu\mathbf L_{FD}\mathbf g^{k+1}. \end{aligned}} \end{equation}\] The first correction accounts for the time variation of the prescribed values through the mass matrix. The second correction accounts for their contribution to the spatial operator. When \(\mathbf g\) is constant, the first vanishes, but the second remains.

Step 4: Factor the reduced system and reconstruct the full state

The matrix \(\mathbf C_{FF}\) is symmetric positive definite. The matrix \(\mathbf L_{FF}\) is also positive definite even if \(\kappa=0\). To see this, for a coefficient vector \(\mathbf v\) let \(v_h\in V_{h,D}\) be its associated function. Then \[ \mathbf v^\top\mathbf L_{FF}\mathbf v =\kappa^2\|v_h\|_{L_2(\Gamma)}^2 +\|v_h'\|_{L_2(\Gamma)}^2. \] If this is zero, \(v_h\) is constant on the connected graph. Since its trace vanishes at at least one Dirichlet vertex, the constant is zero.

Consequently \(\mathbf C_{FF}+\tau\nu\mathbf L_{FF}\) admits a sparse Cholesky factorization. For a fixed graph, mesh, and time step, this factorization is reused at every step. After solving (5), insert the prescribed entries: \[ \mathbf U_D^{k+1}=\mathbf g^{k+1}. \] This is the beta = 1 branch of my.fractional.operators.frac and my.solver.frac. Here C = C[F,F], L = K[F,F], and diffusion = 1 in the present scripts. No rational approximation is needed for this branch.

Harmonic lifting of the prescribed Dirichlet data

Step 5: Define a lifting independently of the fractional power

The homogeneous operator \(L_D\) acts on functions satisfying zero Dirichlet conditions. To introduce nonzero data, let \(\mathcal H g\) be the solution of \[ (\mathcal H g)|_{\mathcal V_D}=g,\qquad \mathfrak a(\mathcal H g,\phi)=0,\qquad \phi\in V_D. \] Equivalently, the lifting solves \(\kappa^2\mathcal H g-(\mathcal H g)''=0\) on each edge, with the prescribed trace and the free Kirchhoff conditions. For \(\kappa=0\) it is linear on each edge.

The discrete lifting \(\mathbf H\mathbf g\) satisfies \[ (\mathbf H\mathbf g)_D=\mathbf g,\qquad \mathbf L_{FF}(\mathbf H\mathbf g)_F+\mathbf L_{FD}\mathbf g=0. \] Since this holds for every boundary vector, its matrix is \[\begin{equation} \label{dir:eq:lift} \tag{6} \mathbf H_D=\mathbf I,\qquad \mathbf H_F=-\mathbf L_{FF}^{-1}\mathbf L_{FD}. \end{equation}\] The inverse denotes a solve with \(\mathbf L_{FF}\), not a dense inverse formed in the implementation. One solves for one column of \(\mathbf H\) per prescribed vertex.

Write the full coefficient vector as \[ \mathbf U_F=\mathbf W+\mathbf H_F\mathbf g,\qquad \mathbf U_D=\mathbf g. \] Here \(\mathbf W\) contains the coefficients of a function \(W_h\in V_{h,D}\); its missing Dirichlet coefficients are zero. Differentiating and inserting this representation into (4) gives \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf C_{FF}\dot{\mathbf W} +\mathbf C_{FF}\mathbf H_F\dot{\mathbf g} +\nu\mathbf L_{FF}\mathbf W +\nu\mathbf L_{FF}\mathbf H_F\mathbf g =\mathbf F_F-\mathbf C_{FD}\dot{\mathbf g} -\nu\mathbf L_{FD}\mathbf g. \end{aligned} \] Since \(\mathbf L_{FF}\mathbf H_F+\mathbf L_{FD}=0\), the spatial lifting terms cancel. Introduce \[\begin{equation} \label{dir:eq:J} \tag{7} \mathbf J=(\mathbf C\mathbf H)_F =\mathbf C_{FF}\mathbf H_F+\mathbf C_{FD}. \end{equation}\] We obtain the equivalent ordinary diffusion equation \[\begin{equation} \label{dir:eq:liftordinary} \tag{8} \mathbf C_{FF}\dot{\mathbf W}+\nu\mathbf L_{FF}\mathbf W =\mathbf F_F-\mathbf J\dot{\mathbf g}. \end{equation}\] The full mass product in (7) matters: keeping only \(\mathbf C_{FF}\mathbf H_F\) would omit the Dirichlet-basis contribution.

Fractional diffusion with the lifted boundary model

Step 6: Specify the continuous spectral problem

For \(0<\alpha<2\), put \(\beta=\alpha/2\). The positive self-adjoint operator \(L_D\) has \(L_2(\Gamma)\)-orthonormal eigenfunctions \(e_j\) and positive eigenvalues \(\lambda_j\): \[ L_De_j=\lambda_je_j,\qquad L_D^\beta w=\sum_j\lambda_j^\beta(w,e_j)_{L_2(\Gamma)}e_j. \] The domain of \(L_D^\beta\) consists of the functions for which \(\sum_j\lambda_j^{2\beta}|(w,e_j)_{L_2(\Gamma)}|^2<\infty\).

The fractional experiments use the following extension of (1): \[\begin{equation} \label{dir:eq:fracmodel} \tag{9} \boxed{\begin{aligned} u&=w+\mathcal H g,\\ \partial_tw+\nu L_D^\beta w&=f-\mathcal H\dot g,\\ w(0)&=u_0-\mathcal H g(0). \end{aligned}} \end{equation}\] Thus the homogeneous spectral power acts on the remainder \(w\). The harmonic lifting carries the prescribed boundary values. This is a choice of nonhomogeneous spectral model, following the lifting approach discussed by Antil, Pfefferer, and Rogovs, here applied to a metric graph. It specifies what the fractional equation means at nonzero boundary data. It is not obtained by taking the all-Kirchhoff fractional power and then overwriting selected entries.

The mixed vertex conditions enter through the realization of \(L_D\). Fractional solutions need not have enough regularity for every classical vertex derivative to exist; the spectral formulation remains meaningful without imposing pointwise flux equations afterwards. For \(\beta=1\), the weak harmonic property of \(\mathcal H g\) reduces (9) to (1).

Step 7: Represent the reduced discrete operator in the finite element basis

Define \(L_{h,D}:V_{h,D}\to V_{h,D}\) by \[ (L_{h,D}v_h,\phi_h)_{L_2(\Gamma)} =\mathfrak a(v_h,\phi_h),\qquad v_h,\phi_h\in V_{h,D}. \] Write its input and output as \[ v_h=\sum_{j\in F}v_j\psi_h^j,\qquad L_{h,D}v_h=\sum_{j\in F}z_j\psi_h^j. \] Testing with \(\psi_h^i\), \(i\in F\), gives \[ \sum_{j\in F}C_{ij}z_j=\sum_{j\in F}L_{ij}v_j. \] In matrix notation, \[ \mathbf C_{FF}\mathbf z=\mathbf L_{FF}\mathbf v,\qquad \mathbf z=\mathbf C_{FF}^{-1}\mathbf L_{FF}\mathbf v. \] Therefore the analogue of \(\mathbf B\) in brasilfunc.Rmd is \[\begin{equation} \label{dir:eq:B} \tag{10} \mathbf B=\mathbf C_{FF}^{-1}\mathbf L_{FF}. \end{equation}\] It represents the operator on the homogeneous, reduced space. One first forms this reduced operator and then takes its fractional power. Taking a block of a fractional power of the full matrix would generally give a different operator.

Step 8: Represent the reduced fractional bilinear form

The finite element inner product is \[ (v_h,w_h)_{L_2(\Gamma)} =\mathbf w^\top\mathbf C_{FF}\mathbf v. \] The identity \[ \mathbf B=\mathbf C_{FF}^{-1/2} (\mathbf C_{FF}^{-1/2}\mathbf L_{FF}\mathbf C_{FF}^{-1/2}) \mathbf C_{FF}^{1/2} \] shows that \(\mathbf B\) is similar to a symmetric positive definite matrix. Hence its eigenvalues are positive, and its spectral fractional power is well-defined. Moreover, \[ \mathbf B^\top\mathbf C_{FF} =\mathbf C_{FF}\mathbf B=\mathbf L_{FF}, \] so it is self-adjoint in the finite element mass inner product.

Define, as in brasilfunc.Rmd, the discrete fractional form by \[ \mathfrak a_{h,\alpha}(v_h,w_h) =(L_{h,D}^{\alpha/4}v_h,L_{h,D}^{\alpha/4}w_h)_{L_2(\Gamma)}. \] Since \(\alpha/4=\beta/2\) and \(L_{h,D}\) is self-adjoint, \[ \begin{aligned} \mathfrak a_{h,\alpha}(v_h,w_h) &=(L_{h,D}^\beta v_h,w_h)_{L_2(\Gamma)}\\ &=\mathbf w^\top\mathbf C_{FF}\mathbf B^\beta\mathbf v. \end{aligned} \] Consequently the fractional bilinear-form matrix is \(\mathbf C_{FF}\mathbf B^\beta\), and the diffusion matrix is \(\nu\mathbf C_{FF}\mathbf B^\beta\). In general, \[ \mathbf C_{FF}(\mathbf C_{FF}^{-1}\mathbf L_{FF})^\beta \ne\mathbf L_{FF}^\beta. \] The operator is discretized first and its fractional power is taken on \(V_{h,D}\), exactly as in the original notebook but with the homogeneous Dirichlet space replacing the all-Kirchhoff space.

Step 9: Assemble the exact discrete fractional time step

Discretize the harmonic lifting in (9) by (6). The semidiscrete equation for its homogeneous remainder is \[ \mathbf C_{FF}\dot{\mathbf W} +\nu\mathbf C_{FF}\mathbf B^\beta\mathbf W =\mathbf F_F-\mathbf J\dot{\mathbf g}. \] Define the boundary-corrected load \[\begin{equation} \label{dir:eq:load} \tag{11} \widetilde{\mathbf F}^{k+1} =\mathbf F_F^{k+1} -\frac1\tau\mathbf J(\mathbf g^{k+1}-\mathbf g^k). \end{equation}\] Backward Euler gives \[\begin{equation} \label{dir:eq:fracbe} \tag{12} (\mathbf C_{FF}+\tau\nu\mathbf C_{FF}\mathbf B^\beta) \mathbf W^{k+1} =\mathbf C_{FF}\mathbf W^k +\tau\widetilde{\mathbf F}^{k+1}. \end{equation}\] The initial remainder is \(\mathbf W^0=\mathbf U_F^0-\mathbf H_F\mathbf g^0\). Equation (12) has exactly the structure of the fully discrete matrix scheme in brasilfunc.Rmd: replace the full mass and spatial matrices by their \(FF\) blocks and replace the original load by (11).

Step 10: Remove the mass matrix and normalize the operator

Multiply (12) by \(\mathbf C_{FF}^{-1}\): \[ (\mathbf I+\tau\nu\mathbf B^\beta)\mathbf W^{k+1} =\mathbf W^k +\tau\mathbf C_{FF}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf F}^{k+1}. \] For the all-Kirchhoff operator with \(\kappa>0\), the original notebook normalizes by \(\kappa^2\). Here \(\kappa\) may be zero, while the mixed operator remains positive. Choose a positive spectral lower bound \(\sigma\le\lambda_{1,h}\), where \[ \mathbf L_{FF}\mathbf v_j =\lambda_{j,h}\mathbf C_{FF}\mathbf v_j. \] Define \[\begin{equation} \label{dir:eq:A} \tag{13} \mathbf A=\frac{\mathbf B}{\sigma} =\frac{\mathbf C_{FF}^{-1}\mathbf L_{FF}}{\sigma}. \end{equation}\] Then \(\mathbf B^\beta=\sigma^\beta\mathbf A^\beta\), so \[ (\mathbf I+\tau\nu\sigma^\beta\mathbf A^\beta)\mathbf W^{k+1} =\mathbf W^k +\tau\mathbf C_{FF}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf F}^{k+1}. \] The parameter \(\sigma\) plays the role of \(\kappa^2\) in the rational normalization only. It does not change \(\mathbf L_{FF}\) and does not add a reaction term to the PDE. The factor \(\nu\) remains outside all spatial fractional powers.

Step 11: Justify the positive scale used by the scripts

Let \(\ell_\Gamma=\sum_{e\in\mathcal E}|e|\) be the total graph length. For \(v\in V_D\), choose a path from a Dirichlet vertex to any point \(s\). The path has length at most \(\ell_\Gamma\). Since \(v\) vanishes at its starting vertex, Cauchy–Schwarz gives \[ |v(s)|^2\le\ell_\Gamma\|v'\|_{L_2(\Gamma)}^2. \] Integrating over the graph gives \[ \|v\|_{L_2(\Gamma)}^2 \le\ell_\Gamma^2\|v'\|_{L_2(\Gamma)}^2. \] Thus, for every nonzero \(v_h\in V_{h,D}\), \[ \frac{\mathfrak a(v_h,v_h)}{\|v_h\|_{L_2(\Gamma)}^2} =\kappa^2+ \frac{\|v_h'\|_{L_2(\Gamma)}^2}{\|v_h\|_{L_2(\Gamma)}^2} \ge\kappa^2+\ell_\Gamma^{-2}. \] We may therefore choose \[\begin{equation} \label{dir:eq:scale} \tag{14} \sigma=\kappa^2+\ell_\Gamma^{-2}>0. \end{equation}\] In the scripts this is operator_scale = c0 + 1 / total_length^2. The generalized eigenvalues imply \[ \mathbf A^{-1}\mathbf v_j =\frac{\sigma}{\lambda_{j,h}}\mathbf v_j,\qquad \operatorname{spec}(\mathbf A^{-1})\subset J_h=\left[\frac{\sigma}{\lambda_{N_F,h}}, \frac{\sigma}{\lambda_{1,h}}\right]\subset(0,1], \] where \(N_F=|F|\). The upper endpoint need not equal \(1\): \(\sigma\) is a lower bound, not necessarily the exact smallest eigenvalue.

Step 12: Rewrite the step using an inverse fractional power

Multiply the normalized equation by \(\mathbf A^{-\beta}\) and use \(\mathbf A^{-\beta}\mathbf A^\beta=\mathbf I\): \[\begin{equation} \label{dir:eq:inverse} \tag{15} (\mathbf A^{-\beta}+\tau\nu\sigma^\beta\mathbf I)\mathbf W^{k+1} =\mathbf A^{-\beta} (\mathbf W^k +\tau\mathbf C_{FF}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf F}^{k+1}). \end{equation}\] Every step so far is an exact rearrangement of the spatially and temporally discrete lifted problem. The rational approximation enters only when we replace \(\mathbf A^{-\beta}\).

BRASIL rational approximation

Step 13: Approximate the scalar power on \([0,1]\)

Use the notation from brasilfunc.Rmd. Let \(B_m\) be the best uniform rational approximation of type \((m,m)\) to \(x^\beta\) on \([0,1]\): \[ B_m(x)=\frac{q_1(x)}{q_2(x)}\approx x^\beta, \qquad q_1(x)=\sum_{i=0}^m c_ix^i,\quad q_2(x)=\sum_{i=0}^m b_ix^i. \] Because \(J_h\subset[0,1]\), the same scalar approximation can be used on every mesh. At the matrix level, \[ \mathbf A^{-\beta}=(\mathbf A^{-1})^\beta \approx B_m(\mathbf A^{-1}). \] On an eigenvector whose \(\mathbf A^{-1}\) eigenvalue is \(x\), these two operators multiply by \(x^\beta\) and \(B_m(x)\), respectively. This is why a scalar approximation gives a matrix approximation here.

The implementation reads numerical BRASIL coefficients from the installed rSPDE tables, interpolating coefficients between tabulated exponents when needed. The best-approximation statements describe the theoretical rational function; tabulation and interpolation are numerical realizations.

Step 14: Change to the normalized operator variable

Set \(y=1/x\). Multiplying numerator and denominator by \(y^m\) gives \[ S_m(y)=B_m(y^{-1}) =\frac{y^m q_1(y^{-1})}{y^m q_2(y^{-1})} =\frac{p_r(y)}{p_\ell(y)}\approx y^{-\beta}, \] where \[ p_r(y)=y^m q_1(y^{-1}),\qquad p_\ell(y)=y^m q_2(y^{-1}). \] The factors \(y^m\) remove all negative powers. Thus \[ \mathbf A^{-\beta}\approx S_m(\mathbf A) =p_\ell(\mathbf A)^{-1}p_r(\mathbf A). \] The source partial fractions have the same notation as in the original notebook: \[\begin{equation} \label{dir:eq:source} \tag{16} S_m(y)=d_m+\sum_{j=1}^m\frac{\rho_j}{y-\eta_j},\qquad \eta_1<\cdots<\eta_m<0,\quad \rho_j>0,\quad d_m>0. \end{equation}\] The underlying structure is discussed in Bolin, Mehandiratta, and Simas (2025), Proposition 3 and Appendix B. In particular, \[ d_m=B_m(0)=\lim_{y\to\infty}S_m(y). \] The source constant \(d_m\) must be retained. It will determine the constant term of the time-step map.

Step 15: Substitute the rational approximation and distinguish the rational state

Replace \(\mathbf A^{-\beta}\) in (15) by \(S_m(\mathbf A)\): \[ (S_m(\mathbf A)+\tau\nu\sigma^\beta\mathbf I)\mathbf W_m^{k+1} =S_m(\mathbf A) (\mathbf W_m^k +\tau\mathbf C_{FF}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf F}^{k+1}). \] The subscript \(m\) distinguishes the rational solution from the exact discrete fractional solution. Define the scalar time-step function \[\begin{equation} \label{dir:eq:Hm} \tag{17} H_m(y)=\frac{S_m(y)}{S_m(y)+\tau\nu\sigma^\beta} =\frac{p_r(y)}{p_r(y)+\tau\nu\sigma^\beta p_\ell(y)}. \end{equation}\] Since both polynomials are functions of the same matrix, their matrix versions commute. Solving for the new state therefore gives \[ \mathbf W_m^{k+1}=H_m(\mathbf A) (\mathbf W_m^k +\tau\mathbf C_{FF}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf F}^{k+1}). \] The exact scalar map would be \((1+\tau\nu\sigma^\beta y^\beta)^{-1}\). Equation (17) approximates this map through the inverse-power approximation.

Step 16: Introduce the transposed operator as in the original derivation

Define \[ \mathbf D=\mathbf A^\top =\frac{\mathbf L_{FF}\mathbf C_{FF}^{-1}}{\sigma}. \] Here the matrix \(\mathbf D\) is distinct from the index set \(D\) of prescribed nodes. From \(\mathbf A^\top\mathbf C_{FF}=\mathbf C_{FF}\mathbf A\), we have \[ \mathbf D=\mathbf C_{FF}\mathbf A\mathbf C_{FF}^{-1}. \] For a polynomial, the similarity factors cancel between consecutive powers, so \(p(\mathbf D)=\mathbf C_{FF}p(\mathbf A)\mathbf C_{FF}^{-1}\). The same holds for the rational function \(H_m\) because its denominator has no zero on the positive spectrum. Hence \[ H_m(\mathbf A)\mathbf C_{FF}^{-1} =\mathbf C_{FF}^{-1}H_m(\mathbf D). \] Set \[\begin{equation} \label{dir:eq:rhs} \tag{18} \mathbf b_m^{k+1} =\mathbf C_{FF}\mathbf W_m^k +\tau\widetilde{\mathbf F}^{k+1} =\mathbf C_{FF}\mathbf W_m^k+\tau\mathbf F_F^{k+1} -\mathbf J(\mathbf g^{k+1}-\mathbf g^k). \end{equation}\] We obtain the analogue of the transposed-operator formula in brasilfunc.Rmd: \[\begin{equation} \label{dir:eq:transposed} \tag{19} \mathbf W_m^{k+1} =\mathbf C_{FF}^{-1}H_m(\mathbf D)\mathbf b_m^{k+1}. \end{equation}\] This identity preserves the order of the factors. It does not assume that \(\mathbf L_{FF}\) and \(\mathbf C_{FF}^{-1}\) commute.

Partial fractions, poles, and residues

Step 17: Determine the constant term of the time-step map

Use the original notation \(z_n\), \(a_n\), and \(r_\tau\) for the time-step poles, residues, and remainder: \[\begin{equation} \label{dir:eq:pf} \tag{20} H_m(y)=\sum_{n=1}^m\frac{a_n}{y-z_n}+r_\tau. \end{equation}\] Each simple fraction tends to zero at infinity. Since \(S_m(y)\to d_m\), \[ \begin{aligned} r_\tau&=\lim_{y\to\infty} \frac{S_m(y)}{S_m(y)+\tau\nu\sigma^\beta}\\ &=\frac{d_m}{d_m+\tau\nu\sigma^\beta}. \end{aligned} \] Thus the constant remainder is \[\begin{equation} \label{dir:eq:remainder} \tag{21} r_\tau=\frac{d_m}{d_m+\tau\nu\sigma^\beta},\qquad 0<r_\tau<1. \end{equation}\] It is not the source constant \(d_m\) itself, and it does not disappear when the boundary values are homogeneous.

Step 18: Identify the time-step poles

Set \(s_\tau=\tau\nu\sigma^\beta>0\), and define \[ F_m(y)=S_m(y)+s_\tau =d_m+s_\tau+\sum_{j=1}^m\frac{\rho_j}{y-\eta_j}. \] Then \[ H_m(y)=1-\frac{s_\tau}{F_m(y)}. \] Consequently the time-step poles solve \[\begin{equation} \label{dir:eq:poles} \tag{22} F_m(z_n)=d_m+s_\tau +\sum_{j=1}^m\frac{\rho_j}{z_n-\eta_j}=0. \end{equation}\] At such a root, \(S_m(z_n)=-s_\tau\ne0\), so the numerator of \(H_m\) does not cancel its zero denominator. In contrast, as \(y\to\eta_j\), \(S_m(y)\) becomes unbounded and \(H_m(y)\to1\). Thus the source poles are removable singularities of \(H_m\), not its time-step poles.

Step 19: Locate the poles and prove that they are simple

Differentiate away from the source poles: \[ F_m'(y)=-\sum_{j=1}^m\frac{\rho_j}{(y-\eta_j)^2}<0. \] Therefore \(F_m\) is strictly decreasing on each interval between its source poles. On the leftmost interval, \[ \lim_{y\to-\infty}F_m(y)=d_m+s_\tau>0, \qquad \lim_{y\to\eta_1^-}F_m(y)=-\infty, \] so it has exactly one zero there. On each interval \((\eta_{n-1},\eta_n)\), \(n=2,\ldots,m\), the endpoint limits are \(+\infty\) and \(-\infty\), giving exactly one zero. To the right of \(\eta_m\), every denominator \(y-\eta_j\) is positive and \(F_m(y)>0\), so there is no additional real zero.

There are exactly \(m\) zeros: after multiplication by \(\prod_j(y-\eta_j)\) the numerator has degree \(m\), and the \(m\) real zeros already found exhaust its zeros. They are simple because \(F_m'(z_n)<0\). Their ordering is \[ z_1<\eta_1<z_2<\eta_2<\cdots<z_m<\eta_m<0. \] This justifies the simple-pole expansion (20) and the negative shifts used by the matrix solver.

Step 20: Compute the residues

At a simple root \(z_n\), the Taylor expansion gives \[ F_m(y)=F_m'(z_n)(y-z_n)+o(y-z_n). \] Using \(H_m(y)=1-s_\tau/F_m(y)\), the residue is \[ \begin{aligned} a_n&=\lim_{y\to z_n}(y-z_n)H_m(y) =-\frac{s_\tau}{F_m'(z_n)}\\ &=\frac{\tau\nu\sigma^\beta} {\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^m \rho_j/(z_n-\eta_j)^2}>0. \end{aligned} \] The source coefficients \((\eta_j,\rho_j,d_m)\) depend on \((m,\beta)\); the time-step coefficients \((z_n,a_n,r_\tau)\) additionally depend on \(\tau\nu\sigma^\beta\). The boundary data affect the right-hand side, not these factorization matrices.

Step 21: Evaluate the partial fractions at the matrix argument

Substitute the matrix \(\mathbf D\) into (20): \[ H_m(\mathbf D)=r_\tau\mathbf I +\sum_{n=1}^ma_n(\mathbf D-z_n\mathbf I)^{-1}. \] Equation (19) becomes \[ \mathbf W_m^{k+1}=\mathbf C_{FF}^{-1} \left(r_\tau\mathbf I +\sum_{n=1}^ma_n (\mathbf L_{FF}\mathbf C_{FF}^{-1}/\sigma -z_n\mathbf I)^{-1}\right)\mathbf b_m^{k+1}. \] Factor each shifted matrix as \[ \mathbf L_{FF}\mathbf C_{FF}^{-1}/\sigma-z_n\mathbf I =(\mathbf L_{FF}/\sigma-z_n\mathbf C_{FF}) \mathbf C_{FF}^{-1}. \] Inverting a product reverses its order, so \[ (\mathbf L_{FF}\mathbf C_{FF}^{-1}/\sigma-z_n\mathbf I)^{-1} =\mathbf C_{FF} (\mathbf L_{FF}/\sigma-z_n\mathbf C_{FF})^{-1}. \] The outer mass inverse cancels this factor in each shifted term. For the constant term, the mass inverse remains. The final solver is \[\begin{equation} \label{dir:eq:solver} \tag{23} \boxed{\begin{aligned} \mathbf W_m^{k+1}&=\mathbf R_{m,\tau}\mathbf b_m^{k+1},\\ \mathbf R_{m,\tau} &=r_\tau\mathbf C_{FF}^{-1} +\sum_{n=1}^ma_n (\mathbf L_{FF}/\sigma-z_n\mathbf C_{FF})^{-1}. \end{aligned}} \end{equation}\] This is the same rational matrix solver as in brasilfunc.Rmd, with \(\mathbf C_{FF}\), \(\mathbf L_{FF}\), and \(\sigma\) replacing the full matrices and their original normalization scale.

Step 22: Apply the solver using sparse factorizations

For each time step, first form (18). Then solve \[ \mathbf C_{FF}\mathbf x_0=\mathbf b_m^{k+1},\qquad (\mathbf L_{FF}/\sigma-z_n\mathbf C_{FF})\mathbf x_n =\mathbf b_m^{k+1}, \quad n=1,\ldots,m, \] and combine \[ \mathbf W_m^{k+1}=r_\tau\mathbf x_0 +\sum_{n=1}^ma_n\mathbf x_n. \] All shifted matrices are symmetric positive definite: for a nonzero vector \(\mathbf v\), \[ \mathbf v^\top(\mathbf L_{FF}/\sigma-z_n\mathbf C_{FF})\mathbf v =\frac1\sigma\mathbf v^\top\mathbf L_{FF}\mathbf v +(-z_n)\mathbf v^\top\mathbf C_{FF}\mathbf v>0. \] Their sparse Cholesky factorizations are computed once and reused for all time steps. The mass solve is reused in the same way. No dense fractional matrix or dense inverse is formed.

Step 23: Reconstruct the state and verify the \(\alpha=2\) limit

After each fractional step, reconstruct \[\begin{equation} \label{dir:eq:reconstruction} \tag{24} \mathbf U_{m,F}^{k+1} =\mathbf W_m^{k+1}+\mathbf H_F\mathbf g^{k+1},\qquad \mathbf U_{m,D}^{k+1}=\mathbf g^{k+1}. \end{equation}\] Hence the prescribed nodal values hold exactly at every stored time.

When \(\alpha=2\), \(\beta=1\) and \(\mathbf C_{FF}\mathbf B=\mathbf L_{FF}\). The solver bypasses the rational approximation and the lifted backward Euler equation becomes \[ (\mathbf C_{FF}+\tau\nu\mathbf L_{FF})\mathbf W^{k+1} =\mathbf C_{FF}\mathbf W^k+\tau\mathbf F_F^{k+1} -\mathbf J(\mathbf g^{k+1}-\mathbf g^k). \] Substitute \(\mathbf W^k=\mathbf U_F^k-\mathbf H_F\mathbf g^k\): \[ \begin{aligned} (\mathbf C_{FF}+\tau\nu\mathbf L_{FF})\mathbf U_F^{k+1} ={}&\mathbf C_{FF}\mathbf U_F^k+\tau\mathbf F_F^{k+1} -\mathbf J(\mathbf g^{k+1}-\mathbf g^k)\\ &+\mathbf C_{FF}\mathbf H_F(\mathbf g^{k+1}-\mathbf g^k) +\tau\nu\mathbf L_{FF}\mathbf H_F\mathbf g^{k+1}. \end{aligned} \] Use \(\mathbf J=\mathbf C_{FF}\mathbf H_F+\mathbf C_{FD}\) and \(\mathbf L_{FF}\mathbf H_F=-\mathbf L_{FD}\). This gives \[ \begin{aligned} (\mathbf C_{FF}+\tau\nu\mathbf L_{FF})\mathbf U_F^{k+1} ={}&\mathbf C_{FF}\mathbf U_F^k+\tau\mathbf F_F^{k+1}\\ &-\mathbf C_{FD}(\mathbf g^{k+1}-\mathbf g^k) -\tau\nu\mathbf L_{FD}\mathbf g^{k+1}, \end{aligned} \] which is exactly (5). Thus the ordinary and lifted fractional derivations agree at \(\alpha=2\).

Initial data, forcing, and the star-graph experiments

Compatible initial conditions

For time-dependent data \(\mathbf g(t)=(1,1/2)^\top\sin t\), \(\mathbf g(0)=0\). Therefore the zero initial state gives \(\mathbf W^0=0\). The same holds for homogeneous Dirichlet data.

For fixed data \(\mathbf g=(1,1/2)^\top\), a completely zero initial state would disagree with the prescribed trace. The fixed-boundary experiments instead use \[ \mathbf U^0=\mathbf H\mathbf g,\qquad \mathbf W^0=0. \] The boundary values are then present at time zero, and the initial profile satisfies the free stationary equations \[ \mathbf L_{FF}\mathbf U_F^0+\mathbf L_{FD}\mathbf g=0. \] This removes the imposed startup jump while allowing the interior to evolve under the nonzero forcing.

On an equal-length star with \(\kappa=0\), let \(c\) be the central value. The harmonic profile is constant, equal to \(c\), on every unprescribed spoke, and on prescribed spoke \(j\) it is \[ u_{0,j}(s)=c+(g_j-c)\frac{s}{\ell},\qquad 0\le s\le\ell. \] At the center its derivative sum is \[ \sum_{j\in D}\frac{g_j-c}{\ell}=0, \qquad c=\frac1{|D|}\sum_{j\in D}g_j. \] For the prescribed values \(1\) and \(1/2\), \(c=3/4\). This describes the initial profile used by both fixed-boundary scripts.

Exact time average of the forcing

For \(f(s,t)=a_f\sin t\), the full spatial load is \[ \mathbf F(t)=a_f\sin t\,\mathbf C\boldsymbol1, \] where \(\boldsymbol1\) includes all mesh nodes. Integrating in time gives \[ \tau\mathbf F_F^{k+1} =a_f(\cos t_k-\cos t_{k+1})(\mathbf C\boldsymbol1)_F. \] The load is assembled on the full mesh before restriction to \(F\). In general \((\mathbf C\boldsymbol1)_F\) is not \(\mathbf C_{FF}\boldsymbol1_F\), because the former also includes the Dirichlet-basis contribution near the prescribed leaves. The scripts use average_sine_forcing(..., omega = 1) to evaluate the time average.

Parameters and files

All six scripts use \(n=6\) spokes of length \(\ell=10\), mesh target \(h=0.1\), \(T=2\pi\), and Dirichlet leaves \(1\) and \(3\). Leaf \(j\) has angle \(2\pi(j-1)/n\) in the supplied embedding. The time grid has \(N=\lceil T/0.04\rceil\) steps and \(\tau=T/N\), so it ends exactly at \(T\). The reaction parameter is \(\kappa^2=c_0=0\) and \(\nu=1\).
