Functionality: BRASIL rational approximation

0.1 Fractional diffusion equations on metric graphs

We analyze and numerically approximate solutions to fractional diffusion equations on metric graphs of the form \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:maineq} \tag{1} \left\{ \begin{aligned} \partial_t u(s,t)+(\kappa^2-\Delta_\Gamma)^{\alpha/2}u(s,t) &=f(s,t), && (s,t)\in\Gamma\times(0,T),\\ u(s,0)&=u_0(s), && s\in\Gamma. \end{aligned} \right. \end{equation}\] Here \(\Gamma=(\mathcal V,\mathcal E)\) is a compact connected metric graph, \(\kappa>0\), \(\alpha\in(0,2]\) is the fractional diffusion exponent, and \(f\) and \(u_0\) are the right-hand side and initial condition, respectively. The Kirchhoff–Laplacian \(\Delta_\Gamma\) acts edgewise and has domain \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:Kcond} \tag{2} \mathcal D(\Delta_\Gamma)= \left\{\phi\in C(\Gamma):\ \phi|_e\in H^2(e)\ \text{for every }e\in\mathcal E, \quad\sum_{e\in\mathcal E_v}\partial_e\phi(v)=0 \ \text{for every }v\in\mathcal V\right\}. \end{equation}\] The derivatives in the vertex condition are directed from the vertex into the incident edges, with each edge endpoint counted separately. We write \(L=\kappa^2-\Delta_\Gamma\) and \(\beta=\alpha/2\). Fractional powers of \(L\) are defined spectrally; the vertex conditions are incorporated through \(L\).

0.2 Numerical Scheme

Let \(V_h\) be the space of continuous piecewise linear finite element functions on a graph mesh indexed by \(h\), and let \(P_h\) denote the \(L_2(\Gamma)\)-orthogonal projection onto \(V_h\). Define the discrete operator \(L_h:V_h\to V_h\) by \[ (L_hv_h,w_h)_{L_2(\Gamma)} =\kappa^2(v_h,w_h)_{L_2(\Gamma)} +(v_h',w_h')_{L_2(\Gamma)},\qquad v_h,w_h\in V_h. \] Its fractional power induces the discrete bilinear form \[ \mathfrak a_{h,\alpha}(v_h,w_h) =(L_h^{\alpha/4}v_h,L_h^{\alpha/4}w_h)_{L_2(\Gamma)}. \] Thus, the spatial operator is discretized first and its fractional power is then taken on \(V_h\).

Let \(t_k=k\tau\), \(k=0,\ldots,N\), with \(\tau=T/N\). The scheme computes \(U_h^\tau=\{U_h^k\}_{k=0}^{N}\), where \(U_h^k\in V_h\) and \[ \left\{ \begin{aligned} \left(\delta U_h^{k+1},\phi\right)_{L_2(\Gamma)} +\mathfrak a_{h,\alpha}(U_h^{k+1},\phi) &=(f^{k+1},\phi)_{L_2(\Gamma)},\qquad \phi\in V_h,\\ U_h^0&=P_hu_0, \end{aligned} \right. \] for \(k=0,\ldots,N-1\), with \[ \delta U_h^{k+1}=\frac{U_h^{k+1}-U_h^k}{\tau}, \qquad f^{k+1}=\frac1\tau\int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}}f(t)\,dt. \] Equivalently, \[\begin{equation} \label{system:fully_discrete_scheme} \tag{3} \left\{ \begin{aligned} (U_h^{k+1},\phi)_{L_2(\Gamma)} +\tau\mathfrak a_{h,\alpha}(U_h^{k+1},\phi) &=(U_h^k,\phi)_{L_2(\Gamma)} +\tau(f^{k+1},\phi)_{L_2(\Gamma)},\qquad\phi\in V_h,\\ U_h^0&=P_hu_0. \end{aligned} \right. \end{equation}\]

At each time step, the finite element solution can be expressed in the basis \(\{\psi_h^i\}_{i=1}^{N_h}\) as \[\begin{equation} \label{num_sol} \tag{4} U_h^k(s)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_h}u_i^k\psi_h^i(s),\qquad s\in\Gamma. \end{equation}\] Replacing \(\eqref{num_sol}\) into \(\eqref{system:fully_discrete_scheme}\) yields \[ \sum_{j=1}^{N_h}u_j^{k+1} \left[(\psi_h^j,\psi_h^i)_{L_2(\Gamma)} +\tau\mathfrak a_{h,\alpha}(\psi_h^j,\psi_h^i)\right] =\sum_{j=1}^{N_h}u_j^k(\psi_h^j,\psi_h^i)_{L_2(\Gamma)} +\tau(f^{k+1},\psi_h^i)_{L_2(\Gamma)}, \] for \(i=1,\ldots,N_h\).

Introduce the mass and stiffness matrices \[ \mathbf C_{i,j}=(\psi_h^i,\psi_h^j)_{L_2(\Gamma)},\qquad \mathbf G_{i,j}=({\psi_h^i}',{\psi_h^j}')_{L_2(\Gamma)},\qquad \mathbf L=\kappa^2\mathbf C+\mathbf G. \] The coefficient matrix representing \(L_h\) is \(\mathbf B=\mathbf C^{-1}\mathbf L\). Consequently, the matrix of the fractional bilinear form is \(\mathbf C\mathbf B^\beta\), and the scheme is \[\begin{equation} \label{diff_eq_discrete} \tag{5} (\mathbf C+\tau\mathbf C\mathbf B^\beta)\mathbf U^{k+1} =\mathbf C\mathbf U^k+\tau\mathbf F^{k+1}, \end{equation}\] where \(\mathbf U^k\) has components \(u_i^k\) and \(\mathbf F^k\) has components \((f^k,\psi_h^i)_{L_2(\Gamma)}\). In particular, \(\mathbf C(\mathbf C^{-1}\mathbf L)^\beta\) generally differs from the ordinary matrix power \(\mathbf L^\beta\).

Introduce the normalized matrix \(\mathbf A=\mathbf C^{-1}\mathbf L/\kappa^2\). Multiplying \(\eqref{diff_eq_discrete}\) first by \(\mathbf C^{-1}\) and then by \(\mathbf A^{-\beta}\) gives \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:inverse_scheme} \tag{6} (\mathbf A^{-\beta}+\tau\kappa^{2\beta}\mathbf I)\mathbf U^{k+1} =\mathbf A^{-\beta} (\mathbf U^k+\tau\mathbf C^{-1}\mathbf F^{k+1}). \end{equation}\]

0.2.1 BRASIL rational approximation

Let \(0<\beta<1\), and let \(\lambda_{1,h}\) and \(\lambda_{N_h,h}\) be the smallest and largest eigenvalues of \(L_h\). These are the generalized eigenvalues of \((\mathbf L,\mathbf C)\), with \(\lambda_{1,h}=\kappa^2\). The interval \[ J_h=[\kappa^2\lambda_{N_h,h}^{-1}, \kappa^2\lambda_{1,h}^{-1}]=[\delta_h,1] \] contains the spectrum of \(\mathbf A^{-1}\).

Using the BRASIL coefficients supplied by rSPDE at exponent \(\beta\), let \[ q_1(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}c_ix^i,\qquad q_2(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}b_jx^j,\qquad B_m(x)=\frac{q_1(x)}{q_2(x)}\approx x^\beta. \] Here the target is \(x^\beta\) directly, and both polynomial degrees are at most \(m\). A fixed approximation on \([0,1]\) covers every \(J_h\) and allows the same coefficients to be used on different meshes. The numerical tabulation or interpolation of coefficients need not give an exact minimax approximation at every requested exponent.

In the normalized operator variable \(y=1/x\), define \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:inverse_rational} \tag{7} S_m(y)=B_m(y^{-1}) =p_\ell(y)^{-1}p_r(y)\approx y^{-\beta}, \qquad p_r(y)=y^m q_1(y^{-1}),\quad p_\ell(y)=y^m q_2(y^{-1}). \end{equation}\] The supplied partial fraction coefficients represent this same function as \[ S_m(y)=d_m+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\rho_j}{y-\eta_j}, \qquad B_m(x)=d_m+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\rho_jx}{1-\eta_jx}. \] The source poles \(\eta_j\), source residues \(\rho_j\), and constant \(d_m\) belong to the inverse-power approximation \(S_m\). They are distinct from the poles and residues of the time-step function derived below. Assuming \(q_2(0)\ne0\), the constant is \[ d_m=B_m(0)=\lim_{y\to\infty}S_m(y)=\frac{c_0}{b_0}. \]

For comparison with the root-product notation, if \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) both have degree \(m\), with roots \(\{r_{1i}\}_{i=1}^{m}\) and \(\{r_{2j}\}_{j=1}^{m}\) counted with multiplicity, then \[ p_r(y)=c_m\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1-r_{1i}y),\qquad p_\ell(y)=b_m\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1-r_{2j}y). \] Thus both products have \(m\) factors, and the corresponding normalization is \(\mathrm{factor}=c_m/b_m\). Define \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:PLPR} \tag{8} \mathbf P_r=\prod_{i=1}^{m}(\mathbf I-r_{1i}\mathbf A), \qquad \mathbf P_\ell=\frac{\kappa^{2\beta}}{\mathrm{factor}}\, \mathbf C\prod_{j=1}^{m}(\mathbf I-r_{2j}\mathbf A). \end{equation}\] This product representation is algebraic; the partial fraction coefficients allow the same derivation without forming polynomial products.

Replacing \(\mathbf A^{-\beta}\) in \(\eqref{eq:inverse_scheme}\) by \(S_m(\mathbf A)\) gives the rational scheme \[ (S_m(\mathbf A)+\tau\kappa^{2\beta}\mathbf I)\mathbf U_m^{k+1} =S_m(\mathbf A) (\mathbf U_m^k+\tau\mathbf C^{-1}\mathbf F^{k+1}), \] where \(\mathbf U_m^0\) is the coefficient vector of \(P_hu_0\). The subscript \(m\) distinguishes the rational approximation from the exact discrete fractional scheme. In the root-product notation, this is equivalently \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:scheme} \tag{9} (\mathbf P_r^\top\mathbf C+\tau\mathbf P_\ell^\top) \mathbf U_m^{k+1} =\mathbf P_r^\top(\mathbf C\mathbf U_m^k+\tau\mathbf F^{k+1}). \end{equation}\] Indeed, setting \(\mathbf D=\mathbf L\mathbf C^{-1}/\kappa^2\), we have \(\mathbf A^\top=\mathbf D\) and \(p(\mathbf D)\mathbf C=\mathbf C p(\mathbf A)\) for any polynomial \(p\). The factors that are polynomials in the same matrix commute; no commutation of \(\mathbf L\) with \(\mathbf C^{-1}\) is assumed.

It follows that \(\eqref{eq:scheme}\) can be written as \[ (p_r(\mathbf D)+\tau\kappa^{2\beta}p_\ell(\mathbf D)) \mathbf C\mathbf U_m^{k+1} =p_r(\mathbf D)(\mathbf C\mathbf U_m^k+\tau\mathbf F^{k+1}), \] and hence \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:final_scheme} \tag{10} \mathbf U_m^{k+1}=\mathbf C^{-1} (p_r(\mathbf D)+\tau\kappa^{2\beta}p_\ell(\mathbf D))^{-1} p_r(\mathbf D)(\mathbf C\mathbf U_m^k+\tau\mathbf F^{k+1}). \end{equation}\] These inverses are well-defined if \(S_m\) is finite and positive on the spectrum of \(\mathbf A\) and its reduced denominator has no zeros there.

0.2.2 Partial fraction decomposition

Consider the scalar time-step function \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:partial_fraction} \tag{11} H_m(y)=\frac{S_m(y)}{S_m(y)+\tau\kappa^{2\beta}} =\frac{p_r(y)}{p_r(y)+\tau\kappa^{2\beta}p_\ell(y)} =\sum_{n=1}^{m}\frac{a_n}{y-z_n}+r_\tau. \end{equation}\] The last equality assumes a reduced denominator of degree \(m\) with simple roots \(z_n\) and \(d_m+\tau\kappa^{2\beta}\ne0\). Lower degrees reduce the number of terms; repeated poles require additional powers \((y-z_n)^{-\ell}\) up to their multiplicity.

Unlike a strictly proper rational function, \(H_m\) generally has a nonzero constant term. Taking its limit at infinity yields \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:remainder} \tag{12} r_\tau=\frac{d_m}{d_m+\tau\kappa^{2\beta}}. \end{equation}\] This time-step remainder is not the source constant \(d_m\) itself.

Substituting \(\eqref{eq:partial_fraction}\) into \(\eqref{eq:final_scheme}\) gives \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:final_scheme2} \tag{13} \mathbf U_m^{k+1}=\mathbf C^{-1} \left(\sum_{n=1}^{m}a_n (\mathbf L\mathbf C^{-1}/\kappa^2-z_n\mathbf I)^{-1} +r_\tau\mathbf I\right) (\mathbf C\mathbf U_m^k+\tau\mathbf F^{k+1}). \end{equation}\] Since \[ (\mathbf L\mathbf C^{-1}/\kappa^2-z_n\mathbf I)^{-1} =\mathbf C(\mathbf L/\kappa^2-z_n\mathbf C)^{-1}, \] we can rewrite \(\eqref{eq:final_scheme2}\) as \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:final_scheme3} \tag{14} \mathbf U_m^{k+1} =\mathbf R_{m,\tau}(\mathbf C\mathbf U_m^k+\tau\mathbf F^{k+1}), \qquad \mathbf R_{m,\tau}=r_\tau\mathbf C^{-1} +\sum_{n=1}^{m}a_n(\mathbf L/\kappa^2-z_n\mathbf C)^{-1}. \end{equation}\] Only sparse solves are required: one mass-matrix solve for the constant term when \(r_\tau\ne0\), and one shifted solve for each pole. For fixed \(h\), \(\tau\), \(\kappa\), \(\beta\), and \(m\), the matrices are unchanged between time steps, so their factorizations can be reused.

0.2.3 Poles and residues of the time-step function

To derive the poles directly from the source partial fractions, set \(s=\tau\kappa^{2\beta}>0\) and define \[ F_m(y)=S_m(y)+s =d_m+s+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\rho_j}{y-\eta_j}. \] The time-step poles \(z_n\) solve \(F_m(z_n)=0\). They are precisely the zeros of the reduced combined denominator \(p_r+s p_\ell\); the source poles \(\eta_j\) are removable singularities of \(H_m\).

Suppose the source poles are distinct and ordered, and satisfy \[ \eta_1<\cdots<\eta_m<0,\qquad \rho_j>0,\qquad d_m\ge0. \] These are assumptions on the selected coefficients, not a guarantee for every numerically tabulated or interpolated BRASIL approximation. On each real interval between the source poles, \[ F_m'(y)=-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\rho_j}{(y-\eta_j)^2}<0. \] Moreover, \(F_m(y)\to d_m+s>0\) as \(y\to-\infty\), \(F_m(y)\to-\infty\) as \(y\uparrow\eta_j\), and \(F_m(y)\to+\infty\) as \(y\downarrow\eta_j\). Thus there is exactly one simple zero in each of \[ (-\infty,\eta_1),\quad(\eta_1,\eta_2),\quad\ldots, \quad(\eta_{m-1},\eta_m), \] and no zero to the right of \(\eta_m\). In particular, \[ z_1<\eta_1<z_2<\eta_2<\cdots<z_m<\eta_m<0. \] These roots can therefore be characterized by real scalar bracketing without expanding the combined denominator into polynomial coefficients.

At a simple pole, \(S_m(z_n)=-s\), so the residue is \[\begin{equation} \label{eq:residues} \tag{15} a_n=\lim_{y\to z_n}(y-z_n)H_m(y) =\frac{S_m(z_n)}{S_m'(z_n)} =\frac{s}{\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m} \rho_j/(z_n-\eta_j)^2}>0. \end{equation}\] Consequently, \(0\le r_\tau<1\), and every shifted matrix \(\mathbf L/\kappa^2-z_n\mathbf C\) is real symmetric positive definite. The mass matrix is also positive definite, so all the solves in \(\eqref{eq:final_scheme3}\) admit sparse Cholesky factorizations. In addition, \(S_m(y)>0\) for \(y\ge1\), and hence \(0<H_m(y)<1\) on the discrete spectrum.

If these sign assumptions fail, real negative time-step poles are not guaranteed. This alone does not establish that the rational approximation is inaccurate or unstable. If the reduced combined denominator has genuine nonreal roots, the real coefficients imply conjugate pole–residue pairs. For a real load vector \(\mathbf b\), their matrix contributions combine as \[ a(\mathbf L/\kappa^2-z\mathbf C)^{-1}\mathbf b +\overline a(\mathbf L/\kappa^2-\overline z\mathbf C)^{-1}\mathbf b =2\operatorname{Re}\!\left[ a(\mathbf L/\kappa^2-z\mathbf C)^{-1}\mathbf b\right]. \] The matrix iteration remains real, but evaluating such pairs requires complex sparse solves or equivalent real block systems; the real positive-definite Cholesky conclusion above does not apply.

0.2.4 Integer power and a fixed diffusion coefficient

For \(\alpha=2\), we have \(\beta=1\) and use the exact inverse \(S(y)=y^{-1}\). Then \[ H(y)=\frac{1}{1+\tau\kappa^2y} =\frac{1}{\tau\kappa^2} \left(y+\frac{1}{\tau\kappa^2}\right)^{-1}, \] so the remainder is zero and the scheme reduces to ordinary backward Euler, \[ (\mathbf C+\tau\mathbf L)\mathbf U^{k+1} =\mathbf C\mathbf U^k+\tau\mathbf F^{k+1}. \] If the physical equation instead contains a fixed diffusion coefficient \(\nu>0\), namely \(u_t+\nu L^\beta u=f\), the same derivation replaces \(\tau\kappa^{2\beta}\) by \(\tau\nu\kappa^{2\beta}\) in the time-step function, its poles, residues, and remainder. The right-hand side remains \(\mathbf C\mathbf U_m^k+\tau\mathbf F^{k+1}\), and the integer-power matrix is \(\mathbf C+\tau\nu\mathbf L\).
